## 2. Content pillars (the recurring "scenarios")

1. **Mission journeys.** A real spacecraft (Voyager, Cassini, New Horizons, Juno, JWST) is the main character, and you follow it as it shows us a place. This is the channel's backbone and where the biggest hits come from.
2. **World profiles.** Deep dives into one planet or moon (*Our Solar System's Moons* series). These are evergreen and work well for search.
3. **Breaking-discovery explainers.** A new image or object gets a calm, full explanation (3I/ATLAS, JWST Neptune). Timing these to the news brings spikes.
4. **Rule-breakers and anomalies.** Objects that seem to break physics or our models (*These Stars Don't Follow the Rules*, *Something Strange Is Hiding…*).
5. **Superlatives and lists.** Most extreme, craziest, largest (*Most Extreme Planet*, *Craziest Things Humanity Has Attempted*).
6. **Compilations and sleep content.** Older videos stitched into 1 to 3 hour runs and podcast audio. This squeezes extra watch time out of the back catalogue at almost no cost.

## 3. Title formulas

| Formula | Template | Example pattern |
|---|---|---|
| Probe POV | What **[probe]** Saw/Detected at **[place/edge]** | What Voyager Detected at the Edge of… |
| Finality | The Final Images We Will Ever See of **[X]** | Pluto & Arrokoth |
| Emotional first person | **[Mission]**'s **[Images]** Stunned Me | Cassini / Saturn |
| Confused experts | **[Telescope]**'s New Images of **[X]** Have Left Scientists Confused | JWST / Neptune |
| Rule-breaker | **[Objects]** Don't Follow the Rules | Stars |
| Hidden thing | Something Strange Is Hiding **[location]** | Beyond our Solar System |
| Superlative | This Is the Most **[adjective]** **[object]** in **[scope]** | Most extreme planet |
| Complete explainer | Everything We Know About **[trending object]** | 3I/ATLAS |
| Trailing ellipsis | What NASA Saw at the Edge of… **Everything** | The ellipsis builds suspense |

**Rules the titles follow:** name a real agency, probe or telescope (borrowed authority); leave a curiosity gap instead of giving the answer; keep it to 6 to 10 words; and sell an emotion (stunned, confused, strange, extreme, final).

**What separates Astrum from the clickbait copycats:** the title is dramatic, but the video is careful and honest. The narrator says what's known, what isn't, and which ideas are only hypotheses. That's why viewers trust the channel and keep coming back.

## 4. Hook anatomy (first 30 to 60 seconds)

Most Astrum openings follow the same 4-step pattern:

1. **Image plus anomaly (0 to 10 s).** Open on a striking real image and state one concrete fact that breaks expectations. Don't start with "Hi, welcome back."
2. **Raise the stakes (10 to 30 s).** Explain why it matters or why it's strange: "And nobody is quite sure how it got there."
3. **Promise the payoff (30 to 45 s).** Tell viewers you'll follow the evidence to an answer, or to the edge of what we know.
4. **Signature intro (45 to 60 s).** Logo sting, then *"I'm Alex McColgan, and you're watching Astrum."* For your own channel, use your own line in the same rhythm.

### Hook types that recur
- **The vanishing/impossible observation:** "In 1671 an astronomer discovered a moon… and it vanished."
- **The last look:** "These are the last images we will ever see of…"
- **The confused expert:** "When the image came back, it didn't match any model."
- **Scale shock:** "A mountain range twice the height of Everest, running perfectly around its equator."
- **First-person awe:** "When I first saw this image, I genuinely didn't believe it was real."
- **Open loop:** "But that's not even the strangest thing about it." This gets paid off around 60% of the way through.

## 5. Structure and retention beats (typical 12 to 20 minute video)

| Time | Beat |
|---|---|
| 0:00 | Cold-open hook (image, anomaly, open loop) |
| 0:45 | Intro line and logo |
| 1:00 | **Context.** What the object is, with Earth-scale comparisons ("about the distance from London to Rome") |
| 2:30 | **History.** Who discovered it and what earlier generations thought |
| 3:00 | *Mid-roll sponsor slot* (Astrum places sponsors early, after the hook has done its job) |
| 3:30 | **The mission arrives.** First close images, building up to the "wow" frame |
| 6:00 | **Mystery #1, tackled with competing hypotheses.** Weigh them like a detective |
| 9:00 | **Re-hook.** "But here's where it gets strange…" introduces Mystery #2, the open loop from the cold open |
| 12:00 | **Edge of knowledge.** What's still unexplained and what future missions could answer it |
| 13:00 | **Reflective close.** A short emotional or philosophical line, a viewer question, then the Patreon/subscribe call |

## 6. Narration and writing style
- Calm, British, understated delivery at about 140 to 150 words per minute, with no shouting or hype.
- First-person reactions ("this genuinely surprised me") make a faceless channel feel personal.
- Numbers are always made tangible: kilometres become Earth places, temperatures become Earth extremes.
- Everyday analogies: bugs on a windscreen, the seam on a walnut.
- He signals uncertainty honestly ("one idea is…", "we don't yet know").
- Paragraphs are short, often ending on a one-line reveal.
- Transitional re-hooks every 2 to 3 minutes: "Now," "But," "Here's the thing."

## 7. Visuals and audio
- **Real mission imagery first** (NASA/JPL, ESA, JWST, New Horizons, Cassini raw images), with slow Ken Burns pans and zooms.
- 3D fly-throughs and renders fill gaps where no image exists (an AI-video pipeline can do this cheaply).
- Simple labelled diagrams, such as orbits, cross-sections and scale bars.
- Ambient and cinematic synth score, with silence used before big reveals.
- Thumbnails (inferred): one striking celestial object on black, high contrast, 0 to 4 words, sometimes an arrow or circle on the anomaly.

## 8. What to copy and what to avoid
**Copy:** the probe-as-protagonist framing, real imagery, Earth-scale comparisons, early sponsor placement, honest uncertainty, evergreen moon/planet deep dives, and compilations of your back catalogue.

**Avoid:** "shouldn't exist" claims the video can't support (the copycat trap), fake "NASA is hiding" angles, AI imagery presented as real photos (label renders as illustrations), and copying Astrum's exact intro line or branding.

**Gap to exploit:** Astrum covers the Solar System's big names often. Lesser-known moons and objects (Iapetus, Miranda, Hyperion, Triton, Arrokoth-class bodies) offer the same "mysterious world" appeal with less competition. The script in this folder targets one of them.
