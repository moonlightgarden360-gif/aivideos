## 2. Why alien content gets a million views

1. **It's the biggest question in space.** "Are we alone?" carries more personal weight than any planet profile.
2. **It gives the viewer permission to believe.** "I'm taking this seriously" lets a cautious viewer click without feeling gullible.
3. **It leaves an open loop by design.** Most alien mysteries are unresolved, so the curiosity gap never fully closes, and viewers argue in the comments. Comment debates are a strong ranking signal.
4. **It travels well.** Alien thumbnails get clicked by people who don't usually watch space content, which widens the audience beyond astronomy fans.

## 3. Conspiracy vs. mystery: where the line is

You're right that the demand is there. But "conspiracy" and "mystery" perform very differently over time:

| | **Mystery** (Astrum's lane) | **Conspiracy** ("they're hiding it") |
|---|---|---|
| Recommendations | Normal | YouTube **reduces recommendations of "borderline" content** that promotes unsubstantiated conspiracy theories. Those videos mostly reach existing subscribers, not new viewers. |
| Ads | Fully monetizable | Higher risk of limited ads |
| Your realistic AI visuals | An illustration of a real event | AI imagery attached to fabricated claims is exactly what YouTube's misinformation and altered-content rules target |
| Audience over time | Trust builds, so they come back | Debunk comments pile up and the channel gets labelled clickbait |
| Example | "The Alien Signal We Never Heard Again" | "NASA Is Hiding the Wow! Signal Message" |

**The playbook:** use the conspiracy *energy* (secrets, silence, "what they don't know", "it never happened again") but make the *claims* real. The best alien mysteries already have true facts that sound like conspiracy material:
- "It was never heard again."
- "Scientists still can't agree."
- "It came from a frequency nobody is allowed to use."
- "It looked artificial."

You don't need to invent anything.

## 5. Hooks for alien videos

- **The artifact:** open on the physical evidence (a printout, a light curve, a photo), then "this is real, and nobody can explain it."
- **The impossible detail:** "It came from the one frequency humans are forbidden to use."
- **The silence:** "We've listened to that spot more than a hundred times since. Nothing."
- **The skeptic's confession:** "I don't believe in aliens. But this one bothers me."
- **The countdown:** "It lasted exactly 72 seconds. Then it was gone, forever."

Always pay off honestly at 60–70% of the way through ("here's what scientists now think…"), then end on the part that's still unexplained. That keeps the comments arguing.

## 6. Topic bank (real alien-adjacent mysteries)

| Topic | The "conspiracy" hook (true) | Honest payoff |
|---|---|---|
| **Wow! signal (1977)** ← scripted | 72-second signal at the "forbidden" hydrogen frequency, never repeated | 2024 hypothesis: a natural flare from a hydrogen cloud; not yet proven |
| **Tabby's Star** | A star dimming by up to 22%, nicknamed the "alien megastructure" | Uneven dust, most likely |
| **'Oumuamua** | A Harvard professor argued it was an alien light sail | Probably a natural fragment, but it's gone and we can't check |
| **BLC1 (2020)** | A signal from the nearest star system, Proxima Centauri | Turned out to be human radio interference, but the hunt is a thriller |
| **Przybylski's Star** | Elements that "shouldn't be there"; one paper floated alien nuclear waste | Probably exotic stellar physics |
| **K2-18b** | A possible biosignature gas on an ocean world | Disputed; better data is coming |
| **US Congress UAP hearings** | Whistleblower testimony under oath | Separate what's documented from what's claimed. Strong, but treat it carefully. |
| **The "Black Knight satellite"** | An "alien satellite" photographed in orbit | A debunk video: a lost thermal blanket from a 1998 shuttle mission. Debunks of popular myths do very well too. |
